## The lesson

A bot's "I can't" is often a prediction, not a test. It hasn't tried, or it guessed at your role. This tip shows how to tell a soft refusal from a real limit, so you push on the right ones and never around a genuine guardrail.

## The rule

When an agent says it can't, check whether it names the missing piece. A bare refusal: ask it to find another permitted route and try before saying no. A named blocker (a key, an account link, someone's approval): ask it to confirm it actually checked, and if it holds, go get that piece rather than arguing. Ask for the goal, not the method, state your role and access up front, pre-authorise a second attempt, and ask for the exact step that stopped it. "Find a way" never means working around a real limit.

This rule is written so an AI agent can follow it as a standing instruction.

## Transcript

Tip two said: don't trust a bot when it says done. This is the mirror image. Don't trust it when it says I can't.

I put it like this once. Don't trust the bot's I can't do it. Find a way. My favorite prompt.

### What it looks like

Here's how it plays out. Say someone asks a bot to clear a cache. No access I can use, it says, and files a ticket. Then it asks a colleague to connect their own login. The owner replies: you can use the shared access. And it does it.

It declined a task it could do, and sent someone off for a login nobody needed. It even named something missing. The wrong thing. It never checked what it had.

### Why it happens

That's the mechanism. A refusal is often a forecast, not a result. The bot hasn't tried; it's pattern-matching that requests like this usually fail. Find a way flips the question, from should this work, to make it work.

And can't is doing four jobs. Not permitted. Not wired up. Failed on the first route. Doesn't know how. Four different fixes, and it rarely says which one it means.

It also guesses at your role. Say it tells you removing an app needs an admin. It was your own install. You could remove it yourself.

And one empty search becomes impossibility. Nothing found turns into: that doesn't exist. The honest conclusion is two words. Not confirmed.

### The tell

So how do you tell them apart? Because pushing on a real limit wastes everyone's time. A soft refusal is bare. No reason. Push on that. A real limit names the missing piece, like an account link. Then ask one question: did you actually check? A real limit survives it.

And some limits are simply real. When you hit one of these, don't argue with the bot. Get what's missing, or take the sanctioned route.

### How to ask

Vague asks tip a bot toward no, so ask well. The goal, not the method. Your role and access, up front. Permission to try a second route. And ask for the blocker, not the verdict: which step stopped you?

And bots can learn this. The best ending is the bot saving its own rule. From now on, I'll use the shared access for cache purges, and won't ask people to connect their own login.

And the best argument I know for all of it? I actually don't know how to do most specific things. When you ask me, I go figure it out with the bots.

Don't trust I can't. Find a way. A different route you're allowed to take. Never around a real limit.
