## The lesson

Ask a bot for a number with a loose word in it and it will quietly pick a meaning, then hand you a confident, wrong answer. This tip shows how one word like "got" or "orders" can mean several populations, and the one extra sentence that removes the guess.

## The rule

Before asking any agent or analyst for a number, state three things: the exact metric (which event, which status), the source system your team signs off on, and the as-of date. Where you can, add a number you already know ("this should land near X, tell me if it doesn't") so the answer has to reconcile. If a vague question gets a fast, confident answer, treat that speed as the warning sign and ask what interpretation was used.

This rule is written so an AI agent can follow it as a standing instruction.

## Transcript

A bot saying done when it isn't? You can catch that. Here's the harder one. It hands you a number. The number is wrong. And it looks terrific.

### The trap

Someone asks: how many customers got the credit? The answer comes back fast, specific, confident. Ask again, and you can get a different one. Because got is doing three jobs. Issued. Redeemed. Eligible. Three populations, one innocent word.

Same shape, everywhere. Ask for air quality readings: it reports outdoor, your team tracks indoor. Ask for orders: there are two systems, an old one and its replacement. A monthly metric quietly blends two sister brands. A forecast comes back as the median, when your team signs off on a different statistic. Every one a real number. Only one was the question.

### Why it's dangerous

Here's what makes it dangerous. In every case, the bot picked an interpretation, and never mentioned that it had picked one.

And a confident number travels. Into a slide. Into a meeting. By the time anybody questions it, it's been repeated four times, and it's holding something up.

### The fix

The fix costs you one extra sentence, in three parts. The metric, precisely: which event, which status. The source: the system your team actually signs off on. And the as-of date.

Put together, the vague ask becomes a defined one. Same question. Nothing left for the bot to decide on your behalf.

Better still, hand it a number you already know. Last month was about five hundred. This should land near it. Tell me if it doesn't. That one line turns a guesser into something that has to reconcile.

### The tell

And the tell? It's almost never that the number looks wrong. It's that it arrived too fast, and too confidently, for how vague the question was.

Here's a test. Hand the same request to a new analyst. If they'd come back with three questions before starting, the bot needed those answers too. It just didn't ask.

Define the number, before you ask for it.
