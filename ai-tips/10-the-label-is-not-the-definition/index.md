## The lesson

The most common mistake in six weeks of bots' self-written lessons: trusting what a field is called instead of what it counts. A number with the wrong definition still ties out and reconciles, so nothing looks wrong and it travels.

## The rule

Before using or explaining a number, find out what it actually counts. Read the definition, not the field name. Treat a surprising number as a likely definition problem before treating it as a story. When the source is unclear, ask the data owner which table holds the number they actually sign off on, and say which dataset every reported number came from.

This rule is written so an AI agent can follow it as a standing instruction.

## Transcript

The bots I work with write down what they learn: a short rule for themselves, after getting something wrong. I went back through six weeks of them.

### Six weeks of learnings

Dozens of lessons. And one mistake shows up more than any other. More than access. More than permissions.

### The pattern

Same shape every time. A number had a name. The name sounded like one thing. It counted something else.

Here's what that looks like. A field called pause date that isn't a pause; it's a pushed-out bill date. Influenced revenue: orders email touched, not orders email caused. Free quantity that goes negative. That's not demand, it's a stale allocation. And a p50 that isn't the number your team signs off on.

One bot put it better than I can. A field name isn't a definition. It learned that after trusting a field called no tax, without reading what sat behind it.

### Why it travels

And that's why this one is worse than a normal mistake. The number isn't broken. It ties out. It reconciles. It's just answering a different question. So nothing looks wrong, and it travels.

Another bot saw it from the other side. A report can tie to the raw data to the cent, and still tell the wrong story.

### The habit

So, two habits. The first is for anyone reading a dashboard. Ask what a number counts before you ask why it moved. A surprising number is usually a definition, not a story. And if the name is doing the explaining, go read the definition.

The second is a rule another bot wrote for itself, and it's the most useful sentence here. Ask the data owner which table holds the number they sign off on.

Because somebody typed that field name once, in a hurry. It was never a definition. It was just a label.

One thing back from you. Think of one number in your world whose name doesn't match what it counts. Reply with it. One line is plenty.

Ask what it counts. Then ask why it moved.
