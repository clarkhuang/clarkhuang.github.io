Read the Math 1A transcript

Berkeley. My very first Math 1A class. The professor looks out at all of us and says we're all stupid American kids. Then came the first midterm. Cs and Ds. For everybody. No curve. It hit hard.

Fast forward to CS150, digital design, one of the hardest upper-division classes there was. Three of us were building a digital circuit, a little MIDI music machine. We took turns in the lab, working all night. Sometimes together. Sometimes alone, whenever we could get in.

And at night, I dreamed in green. Black screens, glowing green lines, like Tron. Dragging AND gates, XOR gates, NAND gates, wiring circuits to burn into a chip.

Here's the funny part. All three of us had the same dream. We were putting every waking hour into that project, and real life just bled into our dreams.

The machine? It mostly worked. I got an A. My partner Pierre, a brilliant straight-A electrical engineering student, got an A-minus. I think it was the only A-minus he ever got at Berkeley. And he was livid. Now, I'm not saying I was smarter than Pierre. He was brilliant.

Cs and Ds in Math 1A. An A in one of the hardest classes on campus. It's all relative, how smart you are. But one thing stayed certain. I just kept going. And I kept doing my best. Silicon Valley C Players. Coming in 2027.

Introduction

I was top of my class in high school. Then I got to Berkeley, a great school, and I landed squarely in the middle of the pack.

That pattern followed me into my career. I've worked at what I'd call C companies. I've done C things, things that seemed like A things to other people. But I know the real truth.

Here's what I've learned. Good is relative, in work and in life. There is always the smarter person in the room. The richer one. The more experienced one. The wiser one. The better father, the better son, the better teacher, the better student.

So what do you do with that? My answer has been pretty simple. Be humble, but stay passionate, and stay curious. Learn deeply, and stay away from superficial things. And yes, sometimes, fake it until you make it.

More than thirty years in software later, from computer science and a minor in music at Berkeley, to founding companies and leading technology teams, one thing has stayed certain. I just kept going, and kept doing my best.

And this book is being made the way I build software now. With AI. It's interviewed and written by AI, from my own words. Welcome to Silicon Valley C Players.

From the back cover

Top of his class in high school. Middle of the pack at Berkeley. A career of “C” companies and “C” things that seemed like “A” things to everyone else, “but I know the real truth.”

Silicon Valley C Players is Clark Huang's memoir of more than thirty years in software: a computer science student with a minor in music at UC Berkeley, a serial founder, and today a CTO building AI-native teams where people and AI agents plan, build, and verify software together.

It is an honest look at what “good” really means when there is always someone smarter, richer, more experienced, or wiser in the room, and at the habits that last anyway: stay humble, stay passionate and curious, learn deeply, and keep going.
