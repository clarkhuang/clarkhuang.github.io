## The lesson

AI usage is the new currency of software. See the spend, the limit and a priced forecast before you start, and remember that owning software costs more than building it.

## The rule

Before any sizable task, show the spend so far, the limit, and a forecast for each path. If you can't see a number, say so, and label an estimate as an estimate. Afterward, record what it actually cost. And before you build something new, check what already exists.

This rule is written so an AI agent can follow it as a standing instruction.

Software engineering one oh one. Lesson nine. Know what it costs.

### What did this cost?

Quick question. The voice you're hearing right now is generated. What did the narration for this whole lesson cost? About four dollars? About forty cents? Or about four cents? About four cents.

Now the harder question. What did the agent time cost, to write this lesson and review it? Honestly? We didn't measure it. And that's the problem this lesson is about.

### A new currency

For most of the history of software, the cost was people's time. And everybody had a feel for it. A day. A week. A sprint. Now a growing share of the cost is A.I. usage. Same idea, different currency. And most teams don't have a feel for it yet.

### Three numbers

So before any big piece of work, a person or a bot needs to see three numbers. What have I spent so far? What's my limit? And what is this going to cost?

And that last number should arrive as a choice. Path one: fix it, and move on. Path two: the full plan, build, verify loop, with a written spec and repeated independent reviews. In lesson one, that cost several times a single fix. Each path gets a price, and what that price buys you.

Then a person makes an educated decision, instead of a guess. It's the conversation we always had. A quick patch, or a proper fix. You decide. Only the currency changed.

### Build, buy or borrow

Cost also decides whether you build something, or buy it. And agents set a trap here. They make building feel free. The tempting thought goes like this. I can build that in an afternoon.

But building is rarely the expensive part. Owning it is. Somebody maintains it. Monitors it. Secures it. Fixes it at two in the morning. That's total cost of ownership. And agents only shrink part of it.

So I still buy, when a tool already does most of what you need. Especially when it carries deep auditing and compliance, beyond the features you'd actually use. And when owning your own would simply cost more.

And there's a third option. Borrow. Open source. Somebody already built that block. It saves you the building. You still own what you run.
