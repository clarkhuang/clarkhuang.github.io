## The lesson

Why a computer science education built on ideas, not languages, still pays off, and why it matters more now that agents write the syntax.

## The rule

When you make a design choice, explain the principle behind it, in plain words. Not just the framework feature you used.

This rule is written so an AI agent can follow it as a standing instruction.

## Transcript

Software engineering one oh one. Lesson three. Principles over languages.

### Quick question

Quick question. I studied computer science at Berkeley in the nineties. What language did they use to teach us object oriented programming? C plus plus? Java? Or a language they invented, just for the class? The third one. It was called Cool. Classroom Object Oriented Language.

You couldn't put it on a resume. And that was the point. The only thing worth keeping was the idea.

### Built it, not used it

And we didn't just use the tools. We built them. A compiler, for that same classroom language. A database, right down to pages and B-trees on disk, and how a query gets broken down to fetch the data. And digital circuits, from logic gates up to chip architecture.

Then whole courses on the ideas themselves. Algorithms. Data structures. Design patterns, and anti-patterns. How a computer actually works.

And it still pays off. When a query is slow, I know where to look first. Without the right index, the database has to read the whole table. Usually, that's the fix.

### Why it lasts

Here's why that lasts. Most of the tools from the nineties have been replaced. The principles haven't moved. Abstraction. Algorithms. Data structures. Design patterns.

Lesson one put this to the test. I had never built a mobile app. I'd never seen that code. But I knew what to ask for. Understand the problem first. A written spec. Tests. A second agent to check the work. None of that is about a language.

### With agents

And with agents, this matters more, not less. An agent already knows the syntax of almost any language. What it needs from you is the rest. What good looks like. Where it's likely to be wrong. When to stop.

### Try it

You don't need four years of school for this. Next time a tool does something for you, ask one level down. How does it actually do that? Ask your agent. It's a patient teacher. Then check what it tells you.

### The rule

Rule three, for your agents. When you make a design choice, explain the principle behind it, in plain words. Not just the framework feature you used.

### Your turn

Your turn. What's one thing you use every day that you couldn't explain one level down? Pick it. And go learn that layer this week.

See you in lesson four.
