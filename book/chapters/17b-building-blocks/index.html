How a result I never set as a goal came out of stacked foundations

The rule · for you and your agents

Build the foundation, not the number. Learn the next step when you need it. Practice the core movements until they are yours, then put them in combination. Keep going. Let the result emerge from the work you stacked, and measure the work you actually did. The same rule holds in life, in tech, in marketing, and in finance: if the foundation is weak, nothing lasting gets built. When you later apply AI to that work, put it on fundamentals you already understand.

Read the chapter

In 2018 I was a CTO at a SaaS company, spending my time building strong platforms for other people, and I realized I had built no foundation for myself. I was not feeling good about my body. I had gained weight without realizing it. I was always tired.

This chapter is that story, in my own voice. It is not a fiction chapter, and it is not Charlie Hack's. The times and places below are from official race results, or from what I have already put on the record, including Instagram posts and reels I made at the time.

A goal I did not plan

In a Dale Carnegie course, a vision-board exercise produced a goal I had not planned: a triathlon. The board also included learning guitar — I bought a guitar and learned a few chords, but that has not gone anywhere yet — and starting to play piano again, which I had learned the fundamentals of when I was young. It talked about some career things, like retiring by the time I was 50, which I missed, but maybe not for long. And then some business-specific things.

I did not make a perfect plan. I learned what the next step needed. In 2019 I joined Orangetheory Fitness and started running and lifting. Within six months I moved to CrossFit, at CrossFit 9 in St. Petersburg, Florida, the gym I trained at from at least October 2020 through 2024. I posted an early reel from a 7 a.m. class at that gym. In 2022 I earned a CrossFit Level 1 trainer certification. I studied the methodology, including its critics.

That gym is where the method started to click. CrossFit is largely gymnastics-focused, and gymnastics is bodyweight movement. Skill and positions come first, then the work you can do with them. Weightlifting is part of that work too. The order I kept coming back to is mechanics first, then consistency, and only then intensity. I was after virtuosity: years of practice, not a shortcut.

That sequence is the method I actually used: not a complete program written in advance, but the next block, then the next one.

Platforms, not numbers

This is the same way I have tried to work in my career. I build platforms and models, not products or specific numbers. CrossFit is an abstraction in that sense: functional fitness as a model of health, rather than a chase for one score. Weak foundations in life, in tech, in marketing, or in finance mean nothing lasting gets built.

The surprise later in this chapter is an example of that, not a contradiction of it. A time I never set as a goal, when I started, came out of the blocks I had already stacked.

Skill over power

A ring muscle-up is one of those blocks. I spent years on it. I worked with a coach. We built the fundamental strengths first. Skill and technique override power and strength. The false grip is a gymnastics technique most people don't know about, and it is part of how the movement works. You don't muscle your way through the rings if the position isn't there. Years later I was still practicing that skill. In a later session I worked strict ring muscle-ups, with the same idea: strict before kip.

That is a building block, the same as a platform at work: you stand on it later.

Double unders, then endurance

Double unders are the same kind of block. You can't do them until you can do a long set of single unders. I finally got them, but they only worked when I was fresh. In the middle of an extreme workout, under fatigue, they fell apart, because I had never tested them under load or under heavy breathing.

The next block was endurance on top of the skill. That is what gets you to unbroken double unders when you are already tired. Today I can do 50, 75, or 100 double unders in a row, unbroken. That took six years. I kept them in the work along the way, including a running-strength session with sets of 25 double unders on the board.

Mechanics first, then consistency, then intensity: a long set of singles, then doubles that hold up fresh, then doubles that hold up when you are breathing hard. The result was never the first step.

A mile, then a half

Running was a ladder too. A mile seemed hard. Then the miles got faster. Then 5Ks, then 10Ks. Everyday runs became a minimum of 10K. Then I was building toward a half marathon.

Finishing a half is one kind of endurance. Excelling at one is another. At the Carlsbad Half Marathon, on January 14, 2024, I ran 1:29:34 official, 89th of 3,395, and 4th in men 45–49. I wrote afterward that the plan that day was to stick with the 1:30 pace group, and that I finished with fuel in the tank, at the end of a 70-mile untapered training week. That is sub-three-hour marathon pace, for a half. It came out of the same stack: the mile that had seemed hard, then the everyday 10K, then the longer work.

Water and the bike

Swimming had its own first block. I was fearful of a couple of laps in a pool. I got to swimming 1.2 miles in open water, with waves.

The bike started as leisurely rides of a few miles. Then 30-mile and 40-mile rides became an average Tuesday. Then, in a half triathlon, I rode 56 miles at a race pace of 20-plus miles per hour.

I did half triathlons. Then I stacked further: a full triathlon. A 2.4-mile swim, a 112-mile bike, and then a 26.2-mile run after that. Stacking, and stacking, and stacking.

Then the marathon, the long way around

My first marathon was the Clearwater Running Festival, in Clearwater Beach, on April 25, 2021. I signed up a week before, without a full marathon-training block behind me. The official time was 5:56:24.2. I finished: 124th overall, and 8th of 8 in men 45–49.

I had thought fitness alone might be enough. It was not, not for that distance, not that first time. A second marathon came after more structured training: the REVEL Big Cottonwood Marathon, on September 10, 2022. Official chip time 3:25:45 (gun 3:27:29). 216th of 1,191, 21st in men 45–49.

The drop from nearly six hours to 3:25 was not a secret workout. It was a foundation I had not built the first time: a structured plan.

The surprise

On April 1, 2023, at 47, I ran the REVEL Mt Charleston Marathon in 3:03:55 chip, 3:04:28 gun, a 7:01 pace. I was 140th of 1,725, and 15th in men 45–49. I had never imagined a 3:03, and I had not set it as a goal when I started. It emerged from the building blocks: Orangetheory, CrossFit, the swim, the bike, the run, and the races that came after. That time was also my Boston qualifier for 2024.

A personal best like that is easy to misread as the point of the work. It was a result. The work was the stack underneath it.

The payoff

On February 10, 2024, at the Mesa Marathon, I was chasing a sub-3 that day and finished in 3:18:43. I had already said so while tapering: whether I hit the sub-3 or not, I was happy with the work I had put in.

On April 15, 2024, I ran the Boston Marathon in 3:43:27 official (gun 3:43:57). I finished 12,784th overall, 8,590th among men, 1,456th in men 45–49. It was, as I wrote at the time, “not my fastest but definitely the FUNNEST.” As I wrote after the race, I just got close to the wall and ran parallel with it for 16 miles.

After Boston I paused that stretch of marathon training. I kept going in other ways. In May 2024 I ran the Dunedin Hog Hustle 5K in 20:23, 3rd male and first masters. In January 2025 I ran the Carlsbad Marathon in 3:44:07. In February 2025 I ran the Publix Gasparilla 15K in 1:05:29.

Still a C player

I competed in the CrossFit Open from 2021 through 2024, in men 45–49, at CrossFit 9. I started where a C player starts. In the 2021 Open I reached wall walks at the cap in 21.1, and in 21.3 and 21.4 I got to the bar muscle-ups and could not do any, as I posted at the time. I qualified for the Age-Group Quarterfinals in 2024, but I did not compete officially. I did the workouts unofficially, and I scaled them, after Boston, and said so that week. I still call myself a C player in CrossFit.

The point is the same one as the rest of this book: I was not the best in the room. I kept stacking foundations, and I kept going.

On December 7, 2025, for my 50th birthday, I did a 50-rep chipper bookended by a 5K run and a 5K row, as I posted that day.

What this is for

Chapter 36, “Sick, Well, Fit,” takes this idea into how a company should use AI. The order is the same: fundamentals first, then consistency, and only then intensity. AI multiplies the foundation you already have. It does not replace it.

The rule I take from this path is simple enough to follow on a Monday, and simple enough to hand to an agent.
