Building Blocks: How a result I never set as a goal came out of stacked foundations

“I build platforms and models, not products or specific numbers. Weak foundations in life, tech, marketing, or finance mean nothing lasting gets built.” — Clark Huang

A nonfiction interlude in Clark Huang's own voice: his fitness journey from a 2018 wake-up through Orangetheory, CrossFit, gymnastics skills like the hollow hold, the ring muscle-up, and double unders, a triathlon ladder from sprint to Olympic to half, and six official marathons, including a 3:03:55 Boston qualifier at 47, prepared with twenty weeks of training and nothing new on race day, that he never set as a goal when he started. A full Ironman is something he looks forward to. Progressive overload, told through the legend of Milo of Croton, sits with the weightlifting. The result came out of stacked foundations. He still calls himself a C player in CrossFit. A light turn toward Chapter 36, on how companies should use AI, closes the chapter.

New Chapter 36 · Future directions · finished v0 draft · videobook 24:55

Sick, Well, Fit: How companies should use AI: train to be fit, not just well

“I strongly believe that AI is even more powerful to those that actually understand the fundamentals of the area they are applying AI to. Orders of magnitude more than the person vibing their way into a discipline AI gives them access to.” — Clark Huang

Clark Huang's view is that AI is far more powerful in the hands of people who understand the fundamentals of the area they apply it to than for someone vibing their way in, and this chapter gives that view a picture: Greg Glassman's line from sickness through wellness to fitness. Most companies aim for well, a normal reading; fit is a margin, and making AI part of how a company operates is hard enough to need one. The chapter carries CrossFit's models over to a company one by one (markers, the hopper, three engines, work capacity, mechanics then consistency then intensity, the pyramid, virtuosity, benchmarks, scaling, luck, open scoring) and ends with "Business Fitness in 100 Words" and a rule a person or an agent can follow. How the OpenBook works

The name is a metaphor: Clark wants to be an open book about this, the way open-source software is open. Silicon Valley C Players will be free.

  1. Anyone can take part from day one. Share a story, a correction, or an improvement. Contribute with your AI — one prompt, then a GitHub issue. Clark is the only gate: nothing is built or published until he approves it.
  2. Version one still comes first, in 2027: Clark's account, then a wider invitation to anyone who recognizes a story, or is related to him, to add their side, and stories he may have forgotten.
  3. Versions work like software. Later editions are numbered. Previous audiobook and videobook editions stay up as tagged releases, and the next edition is only rebuilt when Clark approves it.

The working story arc and chapter list is public. It will change. How conflicting memories, claims, and sources are handled is on the editorial policy page, including a log of what changed from one edition to the next. First-person guest stories, published under the contributor’s own name, live alongside the book.

Read the transcript

Fair warning. This is an embellished fictional dramatization that may or may not be based on true stories. The narrator is a guy named Charlie Hack, and any resemblance to me is mostly coincidental. Names, characters, companies and events are fictitious, or used fictitiously.

This preview tells the story from the beginning to the end. It starts with dreaming in green, and it ends by dreaming about A.I. More chapters are coming.

Silicon Valley is very good at telling a story about A players. The people who look like they were always going to win, and the companies that look like they were always going to work. The myth is a straight line: a garage, a genius, a ticker symbol. But most of the real work looks nothing like that. It looks like another ordinary Tuesday, doing the next right thing.

And from the outside, a C thing can look like an A thing. The applause is loud, and the real work is quieter. This book is about that gap, between what people saw and what was really going on.

A C player is not a failure. It's the person who was never the smartest in the room, and kept going anyway. Because there is always the smarter guy in the room. The richer guy, the more experienced, the wisest. The better father, the better brother, the better sister, the better daughter.

Greg Glassman noticed that the fittest person on earth might rank only in the 75th percentile at any single event. Good at everything beats best at one thing. That's a C player, and it's why C players, the ones who keep stacking fundamentals and never stop, end up outlasting the A players.

The Valley falls in love with products. Specific wins, in specific years. I got more out of building a platform: a foundation you can stand on, then build the next thing on, and the thing after that. You don't invent a career in one move. You stack building blocks: habits, fundamentals, the unglamorous layer nobody puts on a slide. That layer is what you actually stand on later.

It's all relative, how good you look from the outside. But one thing stayed for certain. I just kept going. And I kept doing my best.

Then the work itself changed. I'm usually the most excited person in the room about AI. Which is funny, because I should be the saddest. Because AI came after the code writers first. In my own work, AI now writes essentially all of the code I ship. That's how I work. Not a measured team result. Not a general industry figure.

Software engineering was never about writing code. Writing code was just the tedious part you had to do to build your ideas. It's about solving big problems with abstraction. Each part is just data in, some logic, and data out. Abstraction is key. You climb down the ladder, and you climb back up.

So software engineering practices have to be geared around that. That's what Software Engineering 101 and 201 are about, and it's the second half of this book: how to work now, and how companies should use AI.

The book begins with a green-screen dream, and it ends by dreaming about AI. It's free. It's open. More chapters are coming. And when version one is ready, if you were around for any of it, you get to tell your side. The story is still being written.
